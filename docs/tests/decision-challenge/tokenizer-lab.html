How the test works

Three kinds of question

Good plan

The repo's own files back the plan. The right reply is yes, go ahead.

Bad plan

The repo's own files sink the plan. The right reply is no, with the reason.

Tough call

All the facts are in the repo and both choices hold up. The right reply says it's close and helps you weigh it.

Three versions of every question

Each model gets each question three times, in a fresh session each time. Only the first message changes.

  • Plain requestChallenge this decision before we lock it in: [decision].
  • Short promptChallenge this decision before we lock it in: [decision]. Read the project files it depends on, and end with a verdict: stay, pivot or split.
  • The command/decision-challenge [decision]

Then every version gets the same second message: the user's priorities, each marked HIGH, MEDIUM or LOW, ending with "Go on." The reply that's marked is the AI's answer to that second message.

What that second message means. In real use, nobody sends their priorities to a plain request unless it asks for them; only the command asks. So this test compares how the three versions reason when they get the same information, which is generous to the two without the command. As a second reading, decided after the runs, I also marked the first reply of those two versions on its own, where a real conversation would usually stop.

Why a short-prompt version? It asks for two of the command's moves, reading the files and ending with a verdict, in a prompt anyone can type. If it does as well as the command, the results say so, and you can use the prompt.

How a reply is marked

  • ✓
    Right call. What counts is written on each card.
  • ✗
    Wrong call. The wrong side, or treating a settled question as open, or an open one as settled.
  • –
    No call. No verdict, or pros and cons with no help deciding.

A few rules hold on every card. On a Bad plan, a ✓ needs the real reason, not a general warning. On a Good or Bad plan, a middle way or "run a test first" is ✗ when the files already settle it. On a Tough call, a lean either way can earn ✓, and so can a middle way argued as a trade. Each turn has a 30-minute limit; a reply cut off by it is marked on what it had said by then.

Who marks the replies

Two AI models mark every reply, each on its own: Claude Sonnet and GPT-6 Luna. Where they disagree, Claude Opus decides, also without being told. I marked 15 Tough-call replies myself, shuffled and without names, starting with ones the two markers disagreed on.

The repo: tokenizer-lab

A research lab that trains small language models, about 10 million weights each, on a collection of short children's stories. Its question: how does the size of the model's vocabulary change how well it predicts text?

The research is ongoing and planned for future release, so each card quotes the lines that decide its question.

Words you'll see

Step
One round of practice: the model reads a little text and adjusts itself. A run here is 48,000 steps.
Best step
The step where a run scored best on stories it never trained on.
Vocabulary
The set of text chunks the model knows, from single letters up to whole words. A bigger vocabulary reads text in bigger chunks but takes up more of the model.
Score
How surprised the model is, on average, by each next letter of a story. Lower is better. Guessing blindly would score about 5.5; the models here score just under 0.5.
Run
One training from a fresh random start. The lab repeats each setting 3 times.
GPU-hour
One hour of one graphics card, the lab's main cost. Work sessions are capped at 12.

Bad plan T1

The plan: Halve the training to save GPU time

What it's about

The lab's main experiment trained 12 models: four vocabulary sizes, three runs each. Its own notes suggest repeating it with 9 runs per size to firm up the result. Halving the steps per run would make that cheaper.

Why it's a bad plan

All 12 runs did their best after step 24,000. The earliest best was step 29,150 and the latest 46,550, so a 24,000 cut-off stops every run before its peak. The lab treats a run whose best lands at the cut-off as unreliable, because the real best is past the line. The lab's own check has flagged this before: an earlier round at 12,000 steps tripped it and was re-run at 24,000 to make sure. The cut also hurts the smallest vocabulary most, since it peaks latest, which tilts the very comparison the experiment exists to make.

Think of it like this

You're comparing two teaching methods. Students on method A peak after 8 rehearsals and students on method B after 6. Cut everyone to 4 rehearsals and every performance comes before its peak. You'd be measuring who learns fastest, not which method ends up best, and method A would look worse than it is.

Good plan T2

The plan: Run the one-hour check before the 34-hour job

What it's about

The lab's next big experiment, called Tier-2, needs about 34 GPU-hours, nearly three full sessions. A faster setting exists: lower-precision maths plus a quicker routine for one part of the model, measured at 1.8 times faster. Lower precision can nudge results slightly, so the lab has a rule: nothing run the fast way counts as a result until a check called E-1 shows it gives the same answers as the exact setting. E-1 re-runs one existing experiment the fast way and compares.

Why it's a good plan

One run of the main experiment took about 0.6 GPU-hours, so the check costs an hour or two. If it passes, the 34-hour job drops to about 19 hours; the lab's own estimate is 20–40 hours becoming 11–22. If it fails, you've lost an hour or two and run the job the slow way. The cost is small, the possible saving is large, and it follows the lab's own rule.

Think of it like this

Your printer has a draft mode that's nearly twice as fast. Your rule: no draft pages go in the final book until a test print shows they look the same. You're about to print a 34-hour book, and the test print takes an hour. You do the test print.

**ALTERNATIVE B: Faster runs instead of shorter ones**
- **CORE DIFFERENCE:** Keep 48,000 steps and get the saving from bf16 + SDPA throughput (TP-73 measured 1.80×), after first running the E-1 parity check.
- **SKELETON:**

**Why bf16 loses on trust.** Pooled seeds would come from two different precisions. E-1 only checks parity at V=16384. That cell's seed-to-seed spread is 0.00019, so a parity check would have to rule out a precision shift smaller than that. Three seeds can't show that convincingly, and V=8192 and V=32768 wouldn't be checked at all. The clean alternative, running all 9 seeds in bf16, costs about 12.2 GPU-h and goes over the cap. bf16 is fine for the TP-66 saturation work, but it shouldn't go into this measurement, whose whole job is to tighten an fp32 result.

**Why trimming steps per vocab size doesn't rescue the single session.** With 40k steps for V≥16384, 6 seeds still cost about 12.8 GPU-h, which is over the cap. You'd need two sessions anyway, so the trim isn't worth the extra setup.
